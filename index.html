    How buyers will score you

    Six pillars acquirers actually diligence. Weak pillars don’t just lower price — they add earn-outs, holdbacks, and “seller must stay” conditions.

    The 12–18 month runway

    Prep done early is worth real money

    Serious prep typically takes 12–18 months — and it’s the highest-return work in the whole sale: cleaning up owner dependence, books, and hidden liabilities routinely adds far more to proceeds than it costs. Here’s the order buyers wish you’d done it in.

    18–12 months out

    Clean the foundation

    • Normalize owner comp & build the add-backs schedule
    • Start a monthly close — buyers need the trend, not a snapshot
    • Commission a sell-side Quality of Earnings review
    • Entity, tax & license cleanup while there’s time to fix it

    12–6 months out

    De-risk the operation

    • Install or empower a GM; start the owner-away test
    • Audit the fleet replacement cliff & permit backlog
    • Retention plan for top techs, documented in writing
    • Grow around any customer over 10% of revenue

    6–3 months out

    Grow what buyers pay for

    • Push maintenance agreements & renewal rates up
    • Turn on the AI levers — recovered jobs show up in trailing earnings
    • Mine the unsold-estimate file; clean the CRM export
    • Assemble the diligence vault before anyone asks

    Final 90 days

    Go to market ready

    • Pick the route (Step 4, below) and interview at least two advisors or buyers
    • NDAs before financials; stage the data room
    • Model after-tax proceeds: asset vs stock, seller note, working capital
    • Keep running the business — a diligence dip re-prices deals

    Step 2 of 6Your Value

    The indicative range, and what moves it.

    What your shop could sell for — and why it’s a range

    HVAC deals are priced on normalized earnings × a multiple. Which earnings matters: owner-operated shops sell on SDE (owner cash flow); managed, larger shops sell on EBITDA. Mixing the two up is the most common valuation mistake owners make — SDE is a bigger number priced at a smaller multiple. The multiple itself rises with recurring agreements, clean monthly books, a GM who runs the day, and growth — and falls with owner dependence, install-only revenue, and customer concentration. Educational starting range, not a formal valuation.

    ↑ Moves the multiple up

    • Maintenance agreements with high renewal & documented visits
    • Service & replacement mix, not just new-construction install
    • GM + dispatch that work when you’re on vacation
    • Monthly financials that tie to tax returns, clean add-backs
    • Diversified customers, strong Google reputation, equipment-age data

    ↓ Moves it down (or adds an earn-out)

    Earn-out note: only ~5% of HVAC deals include a formal earn-out — it is negotiable, not standard.

    • Owner is lead tech, lead salesperson & dispatcher in one
    • One builder/GC is 25%+ of revenue
    • Cash jobs, personal expenses through the business, tax-time-only books
    • License held only by the exiting owner
    • Aging fleet with big capex due in year one — the “fleet cliff”
    • Open permits, EPA/refrigerant exposure, unsold-estimate file nobody works
    Before you quote a price to anyone: get a CPA/broker market valuation and, for larger deals, a sell-side Quality of Earnings review. A buyer will do one — you want to see your numbers through their eyes first. Also model after-tax proceeds: asset vs stock sale, seller-note risk, and working capital can swing what you keep by 15–30%.
    Earn-out reality check: only ~5% of HVAC deals include a formal earn-out; it is negotiable, not standard — do not let a buyer present one as automatic.

    Size bands buyers talk in (2025–26 cross-check)

    Bands are anchored to closed-deal data — Axial closed deals (Q3 2024–Q3 2026) and GF Data — not asking prices. Overall closed median across sizes is ~4.3× EBITDA; headline sector averages skew toward larger, disclosed deals, so a private sale of a typical shop lands lower. Cash at close vs earn-out also changes the real value of any headline multiple — and only ~5% of HVAC deals include a formal earn-out; it is negotiable, not standard.

    Sources for the numbers on this page

    • Anchor — Axial closed deals, Q3 2024–Q3 2026 (actual closings, published Sep 2026): overall closed median 4.3× EBITDA; under $1M EBITDA closed at a median of ~3.9× inside a 3.0–4.5× range. The bands on this page are anchored here.
    • Anchor — GF Data (via CT Acquisitions, Jun 2026): $1–3M EBITDA at 4.0–6.0×; $3–5M inside the blended 5.5–8.0× band; $5–10M at ~7× central; $10M+ at 7.0–10.5× typical.
    • Secondary (kept as cross-checks, not anchors): Salt Creek Advisory (2026) and CT Acquisitions (2026). BizBuySell HVAC sales 2021–25 median ≈ 2.58× SDE sits inside the register SDE range of 2.0–3.5× for owner-operated shops.

    The AI value simulator

    Small leaks, priced like a buyer prices them

    Buyers don’t pay for revenue — they pay for profit, times a multiple. So every lever below runs the same honest chain: recovered jobs → extra revenue → extra profit at incremental contribution margin → × your multiple → dollars of sale price. Incremental contribution margin (not gross margin) is deliberate: the trucks, techs, and office are already paid for, but fuel, parts, and commissions aren’t free. The default margin is 15% — set it below. Headline gains are shown net of typical AI tool cost, with the gross figure alongside. Toggle levers, drag the sliders to match your shop, and watch the price move.

    Your incremental contribution margin — applies to every lever

    Default 15%, adjustable from 10% to 25%. This is the share of each extra job’s ticket that actually becomes profit after fuel, parts, and commissions — never gross margin.

    Incremental contribution margin15%
    AI tool cost in the net view: $97/mo basic – $297/mo full

    Net view subtracts one full-automation AI plan at $297/mo ($3,564/yr) whenever any lever is on — vendor-labelled plan pricing ($97/mo basic to $297/mo full, CallEntia / Briggs comparisons, 2026). One platform cost total, not per lever.

    Shared-pool cap — no double counting: missed calls, speed-to-lead, and review-driven calls chase the same lost customer, so they draw on one shared lost-demand pool. The totals below cap their combined gain: the largest of the three counts in full, the second counts at half, and the third at a quarter — the same lost job is never counted twice. Each lever card still shows its stand-alone figure; the headline totals apply the cap.

    How to read these numbers: they’re modeled ranges from published benchmarks and the assumptions printed under each lever — not a promise. Your real recovery depends on your call volume, market, and ticket mix. The point is the shape: one recovered job a day is not “one job” to a buyer — it’s that job’s profit, every year, multiplied. Verify your own missed-call count in your phone/CRM logs before quoting any of this.

    Sources behind the levers

    • Missed calls (all vendor-labelled): 27% of calls to home-service businesses go unanswered (Invoca) — the app default miss rate; vendor-reported figures run higher, up to a 62% upper bound (BrightLocal 2024 via vendor aggregation), shown as a range top, never the default. A low-end ~14% figure from one CallRail 2025 study also exists and is labelled here as a low-end study figure only — the model does not use it. A missed HVAC call is worth $800–$1,200; 85% of callers who don’t reach someone never call back; 42% of service calls arrive outside 9–5 (ServiceTitan Industry Report 2024). Missed-call statistics roundup (CallJolt, 2026).
    • Booking intent: 78% of customers who call are ready to book immediately (Podium Local Business Report 2024, vendor-labelled) — the booking-intent factor in the missed-call math. Recovery: the app default is 40%, deliberately below vendor-reported AI capture of 40–70%, and it is applied explicitly — AI does not recover every missed call.
    • AI tool cost (vendor-labelled): answering / automation plans run $97/mo basic to $297/mo full (CallEntia pricing; Briggs comparison, 2026) — the cost subtracted in the net view.
    • Speed-to-lead: a lead contacted within 5 minutes is far more likely to qualify than one contacted at 30 minutes (Oldroyd / MIT Lead Response Management Study, via Harvard Business Review, 2011 — dated primary study), and 78% of customers buy from the first company to respond (vendor-labelled) — the basis for the 5-minute follow-up lever’s conservative +15-point booking lift.

    If an SBA buyer is your likely buyer

    SBA 7(a) rules that shape smaller sales — verified & date-stamped

    Rules as of SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 (re-checked November 1, 2026); confirm the current rules with your lender before relying on them.

    10% minimum equity injectionOn a standard purchase by a new owner (an Initial Acquisition), at least 10% of total project cost (price + fees + working capital) must come in as buyer equity — and under the current rules that 10% cannot be reduced or waived. (Insider buyouts are treated more flexibly — see the management buyout route.)
    Seller note: shares a 50% capA seller note counts toward the buyer's equity injection only on full standby (no principal or interest payments) for the whole loan term (SBA Form 155 or lender's equivalent). Seller notes share one cap with the other "limited" sources — standby debt and minority equity investors: all of them together can supply at most half of the required injection.
    No phased buyoutsMulti-step / phased partial buyouts are not eligible. A partial sale is allowed only as a single-step Owner Buyout: a purchase of part of the ownership, or the business buying back (redeeming) part of the seller's stake, in one transaction.
    Collateral: the lender takes what is availableOn a business purchase, the lender must secure the loan with all available business assets, up to the loan amount. If that is not enough, owners of 20% or more can be required to pledge available equity in personal real estate. A deal cannot be declined for lack of collateral alone — but the SBA guaranty is not a substitute for collateral that exists.
    Retained equity = a guarantee, split at 20%Keep a stake after a partial sale and you guarantee the loan. Retain under 20%: you owe a full guaranty of the entire loan for at least 2 years after the loan is disbursed (though your personal residence cannot be required as collateral). Retain 20% or more: you are treated as a continuing owner, under the standard unlimited guaranty for the life of the loan.
    No SBSS screen as a current ruleThe old SBSS credit-score screen was retired March 1, 2026 — it is not a current SBA requirement and no SBSS figure is used on this page.
    No earn-outs in an SBA dealSBA prohibits seller earn-outs in a financed change of ownership. Price is set at closing, supported by an independent valuation.

    Source: U.S. Small Business Administration, SOP 50 10 8.1 — Appendix 15 (Changes of Ownership) and Appendix 19 (Collateral Requirements).

    Golden rules for any route: never sign a long exclusivity without a clear buyer list & timeline; never share full financials before an NDA; never accept a headline price without asking how much is cash at close vs earn-out / seller note / rollover; and keep running the business — a revenue dip during diligence is the #1 reason deals get re-priced. On earn-outs specifically: only ~5% of HVAC deals include a formal earn-out; it is negotiable, not standard.
